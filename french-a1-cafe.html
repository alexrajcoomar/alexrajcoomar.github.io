Unité 1 : Au Café: Essential Daily Interactions & Ordering (CEFR A1)

Level: CEFR A1 · Main variety: standard French of France (français métropolitain) · Language of instruction: English · Study time: one or two sessions

How to use this unit. Read Sections 1–4 straight through, out loud where you can. Then close the book and do Section 5 from memory, that struggle is the learning. Only afterwards check Section 6. Everything here is language you could use in a real café in France this week.

1. Learning Objectives

  • Recognise and understand the 20 core café words and phrases of this unit, and follow a short customer–server exchange (spoken slowly) well enough to identify what was ordered and what it cost.
  • Produce in controlled practice polite requests using Je voudrais…, Je vais prendre… and Je prends…, selecting the correct article (un / une / du / de la / de l') in at least 8 of 10 items.
  • Carry out unaided a complete café interaction of 6–10 turns: greet the server, order one drink and one food item politely, ask one question (Vous avez… ? or C'est combien ?), request the bill with L'addition, s'il vous plaît., and close the exchange.

3. Grammar Architecture & Structural Patterns

3.1 Polite Request Formulas (Je voudrais vs. Je veux)

Four ways to order. All four exist in real French; they differ in register and directness, not in correctness.

Je voudrais    + noun phrase     →  Je voudrais un café.
Je vais prendre + noun phrase    →  Je vais prendre un thé.
Je prends      + noun phrase     →  Je prends un croissant.
Je veux        + noun phrase     →  Je veux un café.
FormuleRegister / feelWhen people actually use itA1 advice
Je voudrais…polite, softened ("I'd like")any counter, any terrace, any strangerYour default. Learn it as one block.
Je vais prendre…neutral, very natural ("I'll have")once you have decided, menu in handExcellent second option. Sounds relaxed and local.
Je prends…neutral but direct ("I'm having")common and normal, especially in a hurryFine, just keep bonjour and s'il vous plaît around it.
Je veux…states a want ("I want")with friends, family, children; blunt to a strangerAvoid for ordering. But it is perfectly polite in the negative: Je ne veux pas de sucre.

Two more real-life patterns worth having:

  • Pour moi, un café, s'il vous plaît. — "For me, a coffee, please." Very common when several people order at once.
  • Un café, s'il vous plaît. — Noun + s'il vous plaît alone. Short but genuinely polite in French, as long as you said bonjour first.

TipWhy this matters in a real café:

with Je voudrais + s'il vous plaît you can order anything on the counter without knowing a single verb ending.

3.3 Article Mechanics: Indefinite vs. Partitive vs. Definite

French makes you choose. Ask yourself: one whole countable item, an unspecified amount, or that specific thing?

A. Indefinite: un / une / des → "a" / "some" (countable items)

Use this to order anything that comes as a unit.

MasculinFémininPluriel
Articleununedes
Exempleun café, un croissant, un théune tarte, une cartedes croissants, des sandwichs
  • Je voudrais un café. — I'd like a coffee. (one cup)
  • Je voudrais une tarte. — I'd like a tart.
  • Vous avez des croissants ? — Do you have (any) croissants?

B. Partitive: du / de la / de l' → "some" (an unmeasured amount)

Use this for things you cannot count: water, sugar, cream, and drinks in general.

MasculinFémininBefore a vowel or silent h
Articledude lade l'
Exempledu sucre, du théde la crèmede l'eau
  • Je voudrais de l'eau, s'il vous plaît. — I'd like some water.
  • Avec du sucre ? — With sugar?
  • Vous avez de la crème ? — Do you have (any) cream?

TipThe one contrast to fix in your head:

un café = one cup of coffee (what you order). du café = some coffee, the substance. In a café you will nearly always want un café.

C. Definite: le / la / les → "the" (a specific, known thing)

  • l'addition — the bill (yours, the one that exists)
  • la carte — the menu (this café's menu)
  • le and la become l' before a vowel: le + addition → l'addition.

Scope note: French articles have many more rules than this. You do not need them yet. These three sets are exactly enough to order, add, refuse and pay in a café.

3.4 Negation: Ne … Pas

Wrap the verb: ne + verb + pas. Before a vowel, ne becomes n'.

Je  ne  prends  pas  de café.     →  I don't take / I'm not having coffee.
Je  ne  veux    pas  de sucre.    →  I don't want any sugar.
Je  n'  ai      pas  la carte.    →  I don't have the menu.

The article change after a negation

After ne … pas, the articles un / une / des / du / de la / de l' all collapse into de (or d' before a vowel).

AffirmatifNégatif
Je prends un café.Je ne prends pas de café.
Je veux du sucre.Je ne veux pas de sucre.
Je prends de l'eau.Je ne prends pas d'eau.
J'ai des croissants.Je n'ai pas de croissants.

Le / la / les do not change: Je n'ai pas la carte.

TipIn a real café

this one rule lets you politely refuse anything: Non merci, je ne prends pas de sucre.

3.5 English → French Transfer Traps & Lexical Distinctions

Trap 1: commander vs. demander

These are not classic false friends, but English speakers mix them up constantly.

VerbeSens réelExemple
commanderto order (food, drink, goods)Je commande un café. — describes the act of ordering.
demanderto ask (for)Je demande l'addition. — describes asking.

The trap: you almost never say either one to the server. They describe what you are doing; they are not how you do it. To the server you say:

  • ❌ Je demande un café, s'il vous plaît.
  • ✅ Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît. / Je vais prendre un café.

Also note: demander means to ask, not "to demand." Demander is neutral and polite.

Trap 2: Translating English structures word for word

English politeness runs through questions ("Can I get…?", "Could I have…?"). French politeness runs through the verb form instead.

EnglishWord-for-word (avoid)Natural French
Can I get a coffee?Est-ce que je peux obtenir un café ?Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.
I'll have a tea.Je vais avoir un thé.Je vais prendre un thé.
A coffee to go.Un café pour aller.Un café à emporter, s'il vous plaît.

Trap 3: Gender is not optional

Café is masculine, tarte is feminine, and nothing in the words tells you so. un café but une tarte. Learn the article as part of the word (see Section 2).

Trap 4: Pronunciation: silent endings and liaison

  • prends / prend → /pʁɑ̃/. The -ds and -d are silent.
  • s'il vous plaît → /sil vu plɛ/. The final t is silent.
  • vous avez → /vuza.ve/. Here a silent letter does sound, because a vowel follows.

Trap 5: Adjective agreement

The adjective changes to match the noun: un croissant chaud /ʃo/ → une tarte chaude /ʃod/. Feminine often means a final consonant you can suddenly hear.

Trap 6: c'est vs. il / elle est

  • c'est + a noun, a price, or a general comment: C'est combien ? · C'est un café. · C'est bon !
  • il / elle est + an adjective describing one specific known thing: Le café ? Il est chaud.

At A1, c'est covers most of what you need.

Sociolinguistic & Cultural Protocol

The "Bonjour" Rule

In France, it is customary to say bonjour to staff before you ask for anything, when you walk into a shop, step up to a counter, or first address a server. It takes half a second and it changes the whole tone of the exchange.

Why it matters: bonjour functions as an acknowledgement of the person, not just a greeting. Going straight to the request ("Un café") can come across as treating the server as a vending machine, which is often the real source of the "unfriendly French waiter" story that travellers bring home. Say bonjour, and the same waiter is usually warm.

This is a strong social habit, not a law. People in a hurry sometimes skip it, and nobody will refuse to serve you. But as a beginner, bonjour is the single highest-value word in this unit. Add monsieur or madame and you sound noticeably more polite. In the evening, bonsoir replaces bonjour.

Tu vs. Vous

  • tu = "you," informal, one person, friends, family, children, colleagues you know well.
  • vous = "you," formal singular and plural, an adult you don't know, a server, a shopkeeper, anyone you are addressing respectfully.

In a café in France, vous is the safe default with staff. It is not cold; it is simply the normal, neutral register between adults who don't know each other. This is why the unit teaches s'il vous plaît, vous avez… ? and vous prenez… ? rather than the tu versions.

Both dialogues in this unit use vous for exactly this reason. You will hear tu used freely between young people, in casual settings, and in some workplaces, and someone may well offer you tu directly. Until then, vous costs you nothing.

Note

Informal equivalent, for recognition only: s'il te plaît (tu) vs. s'il vous plaît (vous).

Service & Tipping

  • service compris: "service included." In France, service is included in the displayed price by law. The price on the menu is the price you pay.
  • le pourboire /puʁ.bwaʁ/, the tip. Genuinely optional, and small.

What people actually do: leave the coins from your change, or one or two euros, if you were happy with the service and lingered a while. At a counter for a quick espresso, most people leave nothing at all, and that is completely normal.

This is not North American tipping. There is no expected percentage, no 15–20% norm, and no social penalty for leaving nothing. Your server is paid a salary. A tip in France is a small extra gesture, not a required top-up.

Café Vocabulary in France

If you ask for un café in France, you will receive a small, strong, espresso-style coffee in a small cup. This surprises many English speakers expecting a large mug.

CommandeWhat you get
un caféa single espresso, the default
un café allongéthe same espresso "lengthened" with more hot water, larger and milder
un café filtrefilter/drip coffee, closer to North American coffee, but not available everywhere
un café crèmeespresso with steamed milk, usually a breakfast drink

That is enough coffee vocabulary for A1. If in doubt, un café allongé is the closest easy match to a "normal-sized" coffee.

Section C: Guided Scenario Production

Do these out loud. Record yourself if you can, then compare with the success criteria.

Scenario 1 : Le café du matin (au comptoir)

Situation. It is 8:30 a.m. in a busy café in Lyon. You step up to the counter. The server is friendly but quick, and there are people behind you.

Learner role. You are the customer. You want one coffee and one croissant, and you are taking them with you.

Communication goals.

  1. Greet the server appropriately.
  2. Order one drink and one food item politely.
  3. Make clear that the order is takeaway.
  4. Find out the price.
  5. Close the interaction politely.

Required language (use at least four):

  • Bonjour
  • Je voudrais… or Je vais prendre…
  • s'il vous plaît
  • à emporter
  • C'est combien ?
  • Merci beaucoup / Au revoir

Success criteria.

  • ✅ Greets the server with bonjour before ordering.
  • ✅ Orders both items with correct articles (un café, un croissant).
  • ✅ Uses a polite request formula (Je voudrais or Je vais prendre), not Je veux.
  • ✅ Says à emporter or answers the sur place ou à emporter ? question correctly.
  • ✅ Asks the price and closes with merci and au revoir.

Scenario 2 : L'après-midi en terrasse

Situation. It is 3 p.m. You sit down on a café terrace in Bordeaux. A server comes to your table. You are not in a hurry.

Learner role. You are the customer. You want a tea and a slice of tart, you do not want sugar, and you will pay before leaving.

Communication goals.

  1. Greet the server.
  2. Ask whether they have something (a tea, a tart, your choice).
  3. Order politely.
  4. Refuse sugar.
  5. Ask for the bill and close the interaction.

Required language (use at least four):

  • Bonjour
  • Vous avez… ?
  • Je vais prendre… or Je voudrais…
  • Non merci, je ne prends pas de sucre.
  • L'addition, s'il vous plaît.

Success criteria.

  • ✅ Greets the server before ordering.
  • ✅ Asks one question with Vous avez… ? (or Est-ce que vous avez… ?).
  • ✅ Orders at least two items with correct articles (un thé, une tarte).
  • ✅ Refuses sugar using ne … pas with de (pas de sucre), not pas du sucre.
  • ✅ Requests the bill with l'addition and closes with merci / au revoir.

3Avec du sucre.sucre is masculine → du.
4Vous avez de la crème ?crème is feminine and starts with a consonant → de la.
5Je voudrais du café.café is masculine → du. Note the contrast: un café = one cup (what you order); du café = coffee as a substance.
Answer keyA4: Vocabulary recall
  1. s'il vous plaît · 2. l'addition · 3. merci beaucoup · 4. C'est combien ? · 5. à emporter · 6. Vous avez… ?

Note: item 2 takes l' because addition begins with a vowel (la + addition → l'addition). It stays feminine.

Answer keyA5: Build the request
  1. Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.
  2. Je voudrais deux croissants, s'il vous plaît.
  3. Je voudrais une tarte, s'il vous plaît.
  4. Je voudrais de l'eau, s'il vous plaît.
  5. Je voudrais l'addition, s'il vous plaît.

Explanation: the article changes with the item, un (m.), une (f.), de l' (partitive, vowel), l' (definite: the bill, the specific one that is yours). After a number (deux), no article is used at all: deux croissants.

Section B

Answer keyB1: Make it polite
#Réponse modèleExplication
1Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.Je veux states a want and sounds blunt to a stranger; je voudrais softens it.
2Je voudrais de l'eau, s'il vous plaît.Same change; keep the partitive de l' before the vowel of eau.
3Bonjour ! Je vais prendre un sandwich, s'il vous plaît.Je prends is not rude, but the missing element was bonjour. Greeting first is the key habit (§4).
4L'addition, s'il vous plaît. (or: Je voudrais l'addition, s'il vous plaît.)Noun + s'il vous plaît is genuinely polite in French; bare L'addition alone is not.
Answer keyB2: Which sounds more natural?
#RéponseExplication
1(a)(b) is an English structure translated word for word; obtenir is not used this way in a café.
2(b)commander means "to order" but describes the act, you don't announce it to the server. Je vais prendre is what people say.
3(a)demander = "to ask." You don't tell the server you are asking; you just ask.
4(a)Both are correct French. Vous avez… ? with rising intonation is the most useful everyday form; Avez-vous… ? is formal/written, recognise it, don't chase it.
Answer keyB3: Correct the error
#CorrectionExplication
1Je voudrais un café, s'il vous plaît.demander = to ask, not a way of ordering. Use the request formula instead.
2Un café à emporter, s'il vous plaît."To go" is à emporter, never pour aller (a literal translation that does not exist in French).
3Je ne prends pas de sucre.After ne … pas, du / de la / de l' / un / une / des all become de.
4Je voudrais un thé, s'il vous plaît. (or: Je vais prendre un thé.)"Can I get / obtain…?" does not transfer. French politeness lives in je voudrais, not in a question.
Answer keyB4: Fix the gender or article
#CorrectionExplication
1Je voudrais une tarte.tarte is feminine.
2Vous avez du sucre ?de + le contracts to du; de le never exists.
3Je vais prendre de l'eau.Before a vowel, de la becomes de l'.
4Je prends un croissant.croissant is masculine.
Answer keyB5: Turn it into a question
#RéponseExplication
1Vous avez du thé ?Same words, rising intonation, the most common spoken question form. Est-ce que vous avez du thé ? is equally correct.
2C'est combien ?The standard everyday price question. (Ça fait combien ? also works.)
3Vous avez des croissants ?Plural countable item → des.
4Sur place ou à emporter ?The fixed phrase servers use. Recognising it instantly is worth as much as being able to say it.

Answer keyScenario 1: Example response

Vous : Je voudrais un café et un croissant, s'il vous plaît.I'd like a coffee and a croissant, please.

Why this works: it opens with bonjour before any request (§4), uses the softened je voudrais rather than je veux, gets both genders right (un café, un croissant), handles the takeaway question with the fixed phrase à emporter, and closes with merci + au revoir. Every structure comes from this unit, nothing advanced is needed.
